Fundamentals, with Caveats

Small piece of trivia to start: Norman’s book wasn’t originally called The Design of Everyday Things. The first edition, 1988, was The Psychology of Everyday Things — POET, for short. His publisher talked him into the retitle because “design” sold better than “psychology.” Worth knowing mainly because it’s a preview of the book’s own argument: naming and framing changes how something gets used, even when nothing about the thing itself has changed.

The vocabulary itself is genuinely useful, and you’ll use it constantly after this week, often against your own will. An affordance is what an object’s design lets you do to it — a flat plate affords pushing, a handle affords pulling. A signifier is whatever tells you that affordance is there — a plate has no signifier beyond its flatness, which is exactly why push-doors with pull-handles are the single most reliable design failure on any campus. Feedback is what confirms your action actually did something. Mapping is whether the relationship between a control and its effect matches your intuition — Norman’s classic example is a stovetop with four burners and four knobs arranged in a way that gives you no clue which knob controls which burner, so you’re stuck guessing or trial-and-erroring it every single time.

—

Before Norman, before affordances even had a name in this context, there’s a stretch of about twenty years where people just had to invent the physical vocabulary of “using a computer” from nothing. Worth walking through, because it explains something about why the terms we’re learning this week feel so settled — they were bolted onto a world that already existed.

Start in 1963. Ivan Sutherland, then a PhD student at MIT, builds something called Sketchpad — you draw directly on a screen with a light pen, grab a shape, move it, and it just moves, in real time. People now call this the first real graphical interface, and it predates the mouse by five years.




1968: Doug Engelbart, at Stanford Research Institute, gives what’s now nicknamed “the Mother of All Demos.” Ninety minutes, one sitting, and he shows off the mouse, hypertext, video conferencing, and collaborative document editing — decades before any of those were normal. Engelbart wasn’t thinking “let’s make this easy to use.” His framing was “augmenting human intellect.” Ease of use wasn’t really the point yet. Capability was.

Xerox PARC picks all of this up through the 1970s and actually assembles it into something like a desktop — the Alto, 1973, windows, icons, a menu, a pointer, the whole familiar shape. And then, famously, doesn’t know what to do with it. Xerox’s leadership couldn’t see how any of this related to a photocopier company. Steve Jobs visits in 1979, walks out having seen the whole future, and by 1984 it’s the Macintosh.




Here’s the part that doesn’t usually make it into the potted history: none of this solves the actual problem of getting ordinary people to physically operate a mouse. Moving your hand on a flat desk to move a pointer on a vertical screen in front of you is not an obvious mapping — it’s two perpendicular planes, and nobody’s hand knows how to do that by instinct. Someone had to teach millions of people a genuinely new motor skill, fast, with no manual.

The fix, more or less by accident, was a paint program. A guy named Mark Zachmann — a former Kodak janitor turned programmer — built something called PC Paintbrush, mostly to show off new graphics hardware. Microsoft got hold of it and started bundling it with every mouse they sold, specifically because it was the easiest possible way to get someone’s hand moving a cursor around. Zachmann tested it on kids and had a simple rule: if a kid can’t figure it out, we’ve failed. No manual, no documentation — you just start dragging colors around. That program became MS Paint, and for a genuinely enormous number of people, it’s how they learned to use a mouse at all, disguised as doodling.



So by the time Norman sits down to write his book in 1988, the mouse, the GUI, and a working method for teaching millions of hands to use both had already existed for years, built by engineers with almost none of Norman’s vocabulary. Norman doesn’t invent this world. He names it. Even “affordance” isn’t his word — he takes it from an ecological psychologist, James Gibson, who used it to describe what a landscape offers an animal, long before there was a screen involved, and narrows it down to mean only what a person can actually perceive and figure out how to use.

Which is the caveat worth sitting with going into this week: the vocabulary you’re about to get fluent in is a naming project, retrofitted onto machines a small number of mostly male engineers in a handful of California labs had already built to solve fairly narrow problems — sell some hardware, impress a research budget, make a mouse feel less alien in someone’s hand. Good vocabulary. Not a neutral or inevitable one.
