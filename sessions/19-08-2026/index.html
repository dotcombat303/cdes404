Seven Stages of Action

At four in the morning on March 28, 1979, an indicator light in the control room at Three Mile Island’s Unit 2 reactor went dark, and the operators on shift read that as good news. The light was wired to a valve at the top of the pressurizer — lit when the valve’s solenoid was powered open, dark when the power cut off and the valve was commanded shut. What the light actually tracked was the command, not the valve. The valve itself had jammed open. For the next two hours, the control room told its operators that everything was fine while coolant drained out of the primary system through a valve everyone believed was closed. By the time anyone worked out what was actually happening, the reactor core had partially melted.

Nobody in that control room misunderstood their training. The interface told them something false, with total confidence, and there was no second way to check.

Don Norman has a name for the exact shape of that gap: the gulf of evaluation — the distance between what a system is actually doing and what its interface tells someone is happening. It’s one half of a larger model he calls the seven stages of action, which breaks any interaction into: forming a goal, making a plan, specifying an action, performing it — that’s the gulf of execution, the gap between what someone intends and what the interface lets them actually do — and then, on the other side, perceiving the result, interpreting it, and comparing it back against the original goal. That second half is the gulf of evaluation. Two gulfs, and almost every interaction failure lives in one of them. Getting an unfamiliar hotel shower to the right temperature is a smaller, lower-stakes version of the same structure: forming the goal (warm water), guessing at a plan among an unlabeled set of knobs, performing an action, waiting, and then the genuinely ambiguous business of working out whether that temperature shift was actually caused by what you just did, or just the pipes settling on their own.

Time is doing more work in this model than it first looks like. The gap between performing an action and perceiving its result isn’t incidental — it’s the whole site where trust in an interface is either built or lost. A button with no delay before feedback reads as instantly satisfying. A button with a half-second unexplained delay reads as broken, even when it’s actually working exactly as designed; the interval itself becomes information, whether or not it was meant to be. A loading spinner exists less to communicate anything true about progress than to manage the psychological weight of that gap — to stop the silence from reading as failure.

Space shapes the same stages from a different angle. Whether an interaction happens within arm’s reach, across a room, or at a distance changes what performing an action even looks like, and changes what registers as urgent. A doorbell, a knock, and a shout are functionally the same message — someone is here — pitched at three different distances, and each one carries a different implied urgency purely because of how much space it’s built to cross.
